Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Boston

Routine maintenance makes all the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode inspection every few years, a quick relief valve test once a year, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs like rusty hot water, popping and rumbling, moisture around the base, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can wear out the valve and stress the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Hot Water Headaches Boston Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Boston face a predictable set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Homes on private wells can bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a sulfur smell in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and seismic straps in areas that require them. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less to address early. If your water heater is doing anything unusual, a quick diagnostic visit can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
